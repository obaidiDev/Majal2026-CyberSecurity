Day 1 · Foundations

Security is three properties.
Everything else is detail.

Before tools, before attacks, before a single command — every system either holds three properties or it doesn't. Today you'll see each one: build the mechanisms that protect it, and the ones that break it.

Set the frame: we are not memorising definitions. Each property gets a live demo they can touch. The whole day hangs off these three words: Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability.

Foundations · what the field actually is

Cybersecurity is defending systems against a thinking opponent

Safety & reliability

Survive accident and chance.

A disk fails, a cable is cut, a storm hits, a user mistypes a command. These failures are random and indifferent — they don't adapt to your defences.

Security

Survive an adversary.

A person who wants you to fail, studies your system, and switches tactics when blocked. Close one door and they immediately try the next.

Cybersecurity = keeping information and systems confidential, correct, and available even while a motivated human is actively trying to break them. That one word — adversary — is the whole difference from ordinary reliability. Those three properties are the C · I · A triad — the frame for everything today, and exactly where we go next.
The single distinction that defines the field. Reliability engineering defends against ACCIDENT — random, indifferent, non-adaptive. Security defends against an ADVERSARY — intelligent, intentional, and adaptive: block one path and they find another. Everything this week (tools, attacks, policies) follows from taking a thinking opponent seriously. Then state the goal in one line: keep systems confidential, correct, and available UNDER ATTACK. Don't unpack CIA here — it's the very next slide. Land only: an opponent who adapts is what makes this hard, and different from "the server crashed".

Foundations · the domains of security

One system, many layers to defend — and they nest

← OUTER · the buildingthe data · INNER →
Defence in depth: an attacker needs to beat only one layer; you must not lose any. Perfect code still falls to an unlocked door or a clicked email — which is why the human wraps around every other layer.
Security is not one skill — it is a stack of domains, drawn here outer-to-inner. PHYSICAL (the building, locks, who can touch the machine) → NETWORK (firewalls, segmentation, what traffic is allowed) → ENDPOINT (each device and its OS: patching, anti-malware) → APPLICATION (the code itself: input handling, auth, bugs like SQL injection) → DATA (encryption at rest, backups, access control). The point is not the list — it is that they NEST: an attacker has to beat only ONE layer to get in, while the defender must hold ALL of them (defence in depth). And the HUMAN cuts across every layer — the best-hardened stack loses to one clicked phishing email or one propped-open door. This frames the whole week: each day drills into one or two of these layers. Keep it to ~90 seconds; the concrete attacks come later.

Foundations · where attacks get in

Attack surface vs. attack vector

Two words students constantly confuse, made physical. ATTACK SURFACE = the sum of every point an outsider could reach — here the web server, Wi-Fi gateway, laptop, and phone (4 of 5 assets). The database is INTERNAL: no direct path from outside, so it is not on the surface. ATTACK VECTOR = the single path an attacker actually takes. Press "Trace the attack": a phishing email hits one exposed point (the human/laptop), the attacker logs in with the stolen password — a valid login, not an exploit — then moves LATERALLY, laptop → gateway → database. The payoff to say aloud: the crown-jewel database was never on the surface, yet one vector still reached it through a weaker, exposed neighbour. Then press "Shrink the surface": close the human entry points (training, mail filtering, MFA) and re-trace — the same attack now has nowhere to land. The law to leave them with: you reduce risk by SHRINKING THE SURFACE (fewer exposed points) and by BREAKING VECTORS (segmentation, so one foothold can't reach everything). This is the mental model behind every control in the course.

The C · I · A triad

Three properties a system either has or doesn't

Confidentiality

Can only the right people read it?

Broken by: eavesdropping, stolen credentials, a leaked database. The one you can never undo — once a secret is out, it's out.

Integrity

Is the data exactly what it should be?

Broken by: tampering, forged records, a swapped file. The quietest to break — a wrong number can sit undetected for years.

Availability

Can authorised users reach it when needed?

Broken by: DDoS, ransomware, a full disk. The most expensive event most organisations ever face.

Every control you'll ever configure exists to produce one of these. Every attack exists to break one. When you meet a new tool, ask: which of the three?

Don't lecture the definitions — they'll build each one next. Land only the framing: controls produce a property; attacks break one. Availability is a security property, not "IT's problem".

Confidentiality · first principles · the words

Encryption, in the words you'll use all week

Plaintext in

The readable message, before anything is done to it. Also the word for whatever comes back out after decryption.

Ciphertext out

The scrambled result. This — and only this — is what travels, and what an eavesdropper gets.

Key the secret

The secret input that decides which of the many possible outputs you get. The one thing you must protect.

Cipher the method

The algorithm itself — the recipe. Public, published, studied by everyone. Same cipher + different key = different ciphertext.

Encryption turns plaintext into ciphertext using a cipher and a key; decryption reverses it. The whole field is cryptography. Everything for the next hour is one question: who can reverse it? You'll meet the same three people all day — Alice sends, Bob receives, Eve eavesdrops. They're the standard cast in every cryptography paper ever written.
The vocabulary slide the crypto block needs before a single cipher appears. Walk the diagram left to right once — plaintext in, cipher + key, ciphertext out, cipher + key again, plaintext back. Then the four words, and insist on them, because students blur them constantly: PLAINTEXT is the readable form (it does not have to be text — a plaintext can be an image or a binary). CIPHERTEXT is the scrambled form, and it is the ONLY thing on the wire. The KEY is the secret; the CIPHER is the public method. That split is the entire idea, and it is worth stating twice: you do not get security by hiding the algorithm, you get it by hiding the key — which is exactly what they will prove for themselves two slides from now when 25 Caesar keys fall in ten seconds. ENCRYPTION = plaintext → ciphertext under a key; DECRYPTION = the reverse; CRYPTOGRAPHY = the field that studies both. Introduce the cast explicitly rather than letting the widgets do it silently: Alice, Bob and Eve are the universal convention — A sends to B, E eavesdrops — and a fourth, Mallory, shows up later as the ACTIVE attacker who can change traffic, not just read it. Finally set the through-line for the block: every slide that follows asks the same question in a different way — who can reverse this, and what would they need? Keep to ~2 minutes. Do not preview symmetric vs asymmetric here; that distinction is earned on the next three slides.

Confidentiality · keeping secrets

The oldest idea in security: scramble it so only a key-holder can read it

Let them play with the wheel. The Caesar cipher is the "hello world" of encryption: the KEY is the shift. The point to land: the method is simple, and the keyspace is tiny.

Confidentiality · why a small key is no key

Break it yourself

They break real "encryption" with their eyes. The lesson isn't "Caesar is bad" — it's WHY: 25 keys. Name what they just did — trying every key until one works is a BRUTE-FORCE attack, and it is the baseline every cipher must survive. Then the leap: real crypto keeps the method PUBLIC and the keyspace impossible. That's KERCKHOFFS'S PRINCIPLE — the security must live in the key alone, never in hiding the algorithm. Say why: algorithms leak, get reverse-engineered, get published; keys can be changed, algorithms can't. A cipher nobody is allowed to inspect is a cipher nobody has tested ("security by obscurity"), which is why AES was chosen through a public competition and is the symmetric cipher the whole internet now runs on. Land the contrast with the callout: 25 keys fit on one screen; AES-256's 2^256 do not fit in the universe.

Confidentiality · one shared key

Symmetric encryption — fast, but there's a catch

Press Play: the message visibly scrambles, travels, Eve copies gibberish, Bob unlocks with the SAME key. Say the algorithm's name out loud so they don't leave thinking symmetric encryption IS xor: the real one is AES, chosen in a public competition and running on every phone, laptop and HTTPS connection in the room. XOR is on screen only because you can watch the bits move; AES does vastly more mixing but keeps the exact shape they can see here — ONE key, both directions, and it's fast. Then "show the problem": the key itself must travel, and Eve grabs it. Name it — the KEY-DISTRIBUTION PROBLEM. Make it bite: symmetric crypto is perfect for two people who already share a secret, and useless for two people who have never met. Also make the scale concrete — n people all talking privately need n(n-1)/2 separate keys, so a 1,000-person company needs half a million. For two thousand years this had no answer; the next two slides are the answer.

Confidentiality · a public lock + a private key

Asymmetric encryption — the idea that fixed it

Bob publishes a PUBLIC lock anyone can use; only his PRIVATE key opens it, and it never leaves his machine. Eve has the ciphertext and the public lock, but a public lock only locks. No shared secret ever crosses the wire — this is what HTTPS does on connect. Name it: this is RSA, the actual algorithm, with toy numbers (n = 55) so the arithmetic fits on a slide. Everything on screen is real — c = m^e mod n is exactly what your browser computes, just with an n of 600-plus digits. Open "Show how d is found" to make the security concrete: d falls out of factoring n, so the whole scheme rests on factoring being hard. At n = 55 a child does it; at 2048 bits nobody can. Then flag the crack in it, because the next two slides depend on it: Alice used "Bob's public key" — but she got that key from somewhere, and nothing she has proves it is his. Hand her the attacker's key and she encrypts flawlessly, to the attacker. Encryption gives SECRECY; it does not give IDENTITY. Leave the question open and promise the answer (certificates, Day 3) — don't resolve it here.

Confidentiality · agreeing on a secret in the open

Diffie–Hellman — agree on a secret over an open wire

The magic every student should see once: two people agree on a shared key while an eavesdropper watches every message. Drag either secret — Alice computes Bᵃ, Bob computes Aᵇ, and both land on the SAME number, which never crossed the wire. Eve has A and B but would have to solve the discrete log to recover a secret. This is what happens the instant every HTTPS page loads. Now the qualifier that matters, and it is the reason attackers still win: notice WHAT KIND of attacker Eve is here. She only listens — a PASSIVE eavesdropper — and against her this is unbreakable. An ACTIVE attacker who can also modify traffic beats it easily: she runs the whole exchange twice, playing Bob to Alice and Alice to Bob, ends up with one shared key on each side, and relays everything in clear while both parties see a perfectly encrypted connection. That is a MAN-IN-THE-MIDDLE, and neither Diffie–Hellman nor RSA can stop it, because the maths never checks WHO is at the other end. Passive vs active is a distinction they should carry all week. Same open question as the last slide — identity — and the same answer: certificates, on Day 3, where they will run the attack themselves.

Putting the crypto together · how HTTPS moves the key

Asymmetric moves the key, symmetric the data

RSA is too slow and too small to encrypt a message directly, so we never do. Step through it: Alice encrypts the body with a fast one-time symmetric key K, wraps just K with Bob's public key, and sends both. Only Bob's private key unwraps K. This is the payoff of the last three slides: asymmetric solved key distribution but is slow and size-limited; symmetric is fast and unlimited but needs a shared key. Hybrid uses each for the one job it's good at, and it is what the padlock in the address bar actually is. Then be honest about what the padlock does NOT prove, since it's the myth most students carry: the whole envelope assumed Bob's public key really was Bob's. An attacker who substitutes their own key gets a flawlessly encrypted connection to themselves, and the browser would show a lock. HTTPS solves two problems — secrecy AND identity — and only the first one is on this slide. Day 3 opens a real certificate. The next slide adds the signature: proof of WHO sent it.

Putting the crypto together · sign it, seal it

Encryption hides it. A signature proves who sent it. You need both.

Play the attacker. Encrypt (Bob's public key) buys secrecy; Sign (Alice's private key over a small fingerprint of the message) buys authenticity + integrity. Read is stopped only by Encrypt; Tamper and Impersonate are stopped only by Sign. The killer point: encryption alone does NOT prove the sender — anyone has Bob's public key — which is exactly why signatures exist. The "fingerprint" here is a toy; the real one is the SHA-256 hash they'll meet in the next block. Close by naming the third property a signature buys, because it's the one that has legal weight: NON-REPUDIATION. Only Alice holds her private key, so a valid signature is something she cannot later deny producing — unlike a shared password or a shared symmetric key, where either party could have written the message and neither can prove which. That is precisely why signatures sit under contracts, software updates and bank transfers, and it is worth stating that a signature is the one crypto primitive that produces evidence rather than protection.

Integrity · first principles · three things people confuse

Encoding, encryption, hashing — three different jobs

All three turn readable input into unreadable output. Only one of them is not meant to be undone.

Encoding not security

Reversible by anyone. No key. Its job is compatibility — packing data into a form some system can carry safely.

Base64, URL-encoding. Anyone can decode it in one command. If a system "encodes" your password, it has protected nothing.

Encryption confidentiality

Reversible — but only with the key. Its job is secrecy: keep the content readable to key-holders and useless to everyone else.

AES, RSA. What you just spent the last hour building.

Hashing integrity

Not reversible at all. No key. Its job is proof: a short fingerprint that changes if the input changes by even one bit.

SHA-256. Any input, any size — always the same fixed-size output.
The question that separates them: who can get the original back? Encoding — anyone. Encryption — whoever holds the key. Hashing — nobody, ever, by design. So when someone says "the passwords in our database are encrypted", that is the wrong answer, and worth catching: encrypted means there's a key somewhere that turns them back into passwords. Stored passwords should be hashed — see the next three slides.
The single most common confusion in the whole subject, and cheap to fix if you fix it before the hash demos rather than after. Run the three cards as one question: WHO CAN GET THE ORIGINAL BACK? ENCODING — anybody, because there is no secret involved at all; Base64 exists so that binary data can survive a channel that only carries text, and calling it security is a genuine, frequent, real-world mistake (students will meet Base64 in Day 4's web work and should never mistake it for protection). ENCRYPTION — only the key-holder; the transformation is designed to be undone, by exactly one party. HASHING — nobody. Not the attacker, not the server, not the person who wrote it. That is not a limitation, it is the entire point, and it is why a hash can be published safely next to the file it describes. Land the practical test they can use for the rest of their careers: if you can get the input back, it was never a hash. Then the pointed example in the callout — "our passwords are encrypted" is a red flag, because it means a key exists that reverses them, and whoever steals the database will look for that key too. Hashed passwords have no such key. That sets up the next three slides exactly: fingerprint → tamper check → password storage. Two minutes, then straight into the avalanche.

Integrity · proof that nothing changed

The fingerprint that shatters if you touch one letter

The single best crypto "aha": type, then change one character and watch ~half the fingerprint flip. A hash is a one-way fingerprint. This is the machine behind every integrity check for the rest of the week. Correct the confusion before it forms: hashing is NOT encryption. There is no key and there is no way back — that is the feature, not a limitation. Encryption is designed to be reversed by the key-holder; a hash is designed so that nobody, ever, can reverse it. Walk the five properties under the grid, and spend the extra breath on the fifth: COLLISION RESISTANCE — it must be infeasible to find two different inputs with the same fingerprint. Say why it carries everything else: if you can manufacture a collision, you can swap a signed contract for a different one that hashes identically, and every signature and checksum built on that hash becomes worthless. Then the part students find sobering — these properties EXPIRE. MD5 and SHA-1 were the standard for years until researchers learned to produce collisions on demand; both are now unusable for security, and anything still relying on them is broken. SHA-256 is today's answer, and one day it won't be. Crypto is not a fact you learn once, it is a moving frontier.

Integrity · did this file get tampered with?

Same fingerprint = same file. Different = don't trust it.

This is the everyday use of a hash: verify a download, a log, an update wasn't swapped. Have them edit one byte and watch VERIFIED flip to TAMPERED. Integrity is about correctness, not secrecy.

Integrity · how your password is stored

Servers verify your password without ever knowing it

Three ideas here, and the third is the one most courses leave out. (1) The server stores a HASH, not the password — it verifies without ever knowing it, which is why a real service can never email you your password, only reset it. If one ever emails it back, they stored it in the clear. (2) SALTING: a unique random value per user, hashed together with the password. Toggle it live — same password, completely different stored hashes. Without it, identical hashes reveal which users share a password, and one precomputed RAINBOW TABLE cracks every common password across the whole database at once. With it, precomputation is useless and the attacker must attack each account separately. Note the salt is NOT secret; it is stored right next to the hash. Its job is uniqueness, not secrecy. (3) The correction on the callout: SHA-256 is a poor choice for passwords precisely because it is FAST — a rented GPU tries billions of candidates a second, and speed is the attacker's asset here. Real password storage uses a deliberately slow, memory-hard function — bcrypt, scrypt, or Argon2 (today's default). They salt for you and expose a tunable WORK FACTOR you turn up as hardware gets faster: one login costs a legitimate user a fraction of a second, while cracking a stolen database costs the attacker centuries. So the rule to leave them with is not "hash your passwords" — it is "never design this yourself; use Argon2 or bcrypt."

Availability · first principles · what breaks it, what defends it

Availability breaks for boring reasons, not just attacks

Flooding deliberate

More requests than you can answer, so real users get nothing. DoS = one source; DDoS = thousands at once.

Ransomware deliberate

Your data is still there and still yours — encrypted, and you don't have the key. Same property, no traffic involved.

Failure accident

A dead disk, a full disk, a cut cable, a power cut, an expired certificate. No attacker required.

Us accident

A bad deploy, a wrong firewall rule, a mistyped command. Statistically the most common outage of all.

The four defences — the next slide's controls, by name:

Rate limiting

Cap how many requests one source may make per second. Cheap junk gets refused before it costs you real work.

Filtering

Recognise attack traffic by its pattern and drop it upstream — ideally on someone else's much bigger network.

Scaling out

More servers sharing the load, so the same flood is spread thinner and no one machine is the service.

Backups

The only one that still works after the data is already encrypted or gone. Untested backup, no backup.

Two corrections to the instinct students arrive with. FIRST: availability is not a synonym for DDoS. The top row is deliberately half attack and half accident — flooding and ransomware are adversarial, but dead hardware, full disks, expired certificates and bad deploys take far more services down in practice than attackers do, and a mistyped command by your own team is statistically the most likely outage you will ever cause. That is why availability sits inside security at all: the property is "authorised users can reach it", and the property does not care why they can't. SECOND: name the controls before they meet them as unlabelled checkboxes on the next slide. RATE LIMITING caps requests per source per second — the key insight is that it refuses cheap traffic cheaply, before it costs you a database query. FILTERING recognises attack traffic by pattern and drops it, and for volumetric floods it must happen UPSTREAM, on a provider's network far bigger than your own — you cannot filter a flood that has already saturated your cable. SCALING OUT spreads load across more machines so no single one is the whole service; it buys headroom, not immunity, and the attacker can always rent more. BACKUPS are the odd one out and the most important: they are the only control on this slide that still helps AFTER the damage, which is exactly why they are the ransomware answer. Say the rule aloud — a backup you have never restored from is not a backup, it is a hope. Then go straight to the simulation and have them drive the first three.

Availability · reachable when it matters

Nothing stolen. Nothing changed. Nobody gets in.

The property people forget is a security property. Drive the attack slider up and watch real users get dropped; then show the two defences recover it. Tie it to ransomware: same property, different weapon. Then explain what the red dots actually ARE, because the toy shows the flood without saying where it comes from. A DoS is one machine exhausting a finite resource — bandwidth, connections, CPU — and it is easy to stop: find the source address, block it, done. The extra D changes everything. A DDoS is DISTRIBUTED across a BOTNET: thousands or millions of hijacked machines — home routers, IP cameras, DVRs, compromised PCs — each sending modest, individually plausible traffic from a different address. There is no single plug to pull, and you cannot block "everyone", so the defences are the two on screen: FILTER (rate limiting, upstream scrubbing, a CDN absorbing it) and CAPACITY (scale out, so the flood costs them more than it costs you). Worth naming the ugly asymmetry: the attacker rents a botnet for a few dollars an hour; you pay for the bandwidth to survive it. And note those botnet nodes are victims too — this is where "patch your router" stops sounding like nagging.

Putting it together

Which property just broke?

The reflex that outlasts every tool. For each incident, which of C/I/A failed? Two cards break more than one property — that's the point of the exercise, not a trick. Whatever tool they meet all week, this question comes first. Spend a moment on the last card and put it next to card 2. Card 2 is textbook ransomware: files encrypted, nothing taken, pure AVAILABILITY. The last card is what ransomware gangs actually do now — copy the data out first, THEN encrypt: "pay us, or we publish." That is double extortion, and it breaks CONFIDENTIALITY and AVAILABILITY together. Why it matters practically: backups defeat the availability half completely, which is exactly why the criminals added the confidentiality half — against a stolen copy, restoring from backup changes nothing. So the honest lesson is that no single control covers a compound attack, and "we have backups" stopped being a complete answer to ransomware years ago.

Reality check · how break-ins actually begin

They don't break in — they log in

Exploiting code is rare and gets patched. A valid password is cheap and quiet. Where it comes from:

Phishing

A fake login page.

Breach dumps

Billions leaked, free.

Weak & guessed

Cracked in seconds.

Reuse is the multiplier — one leak opens every account. That's credential stuffing.
Movie myth, busted in a line: intrusions are logins, not exploits — cheaper and quieter. The three cards are just WHERE the password comes from: phishing, breach dumps, weak-guessing. The engine that turns one leak into many is password reuse — credential stuffing, replayed across thousands of sites automatically. It's the most common attack on the internet. Keep it fast; the fix is next.

Identity · the two questions every system asks

Who are you? — and what may you do?

Every login is really two checks, and they fail in completely different ways.

Authentication · authN

Who are you?

Proving the claim: a password, a code from your phone, a fingerprint, a certificate. Break this and a stranger becomes you.

Authorisation · authZ

What may you do?

Deciding what that identity may touch: roles, permissions, which records, which server. Break this and any user becomes an admin.

The phished employee you watched earlier passed authentication perfectly — the stolen password was valid, so the system had no reason to refuse. What let one laptop reach the crown-jewel database was weak authorisation. That is why least privilege is a control, not a nicety. A third A usually rides along: accounting — logging who did what, so you can answer the question afterwards. Together: AAA.
Two words used interchangeably in conversation and never interchangeable in a system. AUTHENTICATION (authN) = proving identity: you claim to be someone, and you back the claim with evidence — a password, an MFA code, a fingerprint, a client certificate. AUTHORISATION (authZ) = what that proven identity is permitted to do: read this record, restart that server, view this folder. The order matters: you authenticate FIRST, then every subsequent request is an authorisation decision. The reason to teach them as a pair is that they fail differently and are fixed differently. An authN failure means an outsider gets in as someone. An authZ failure means someone who legitimately logged in reaches things they should never touch — and note that MFA, password managers, none of it helps you there, because the attacker IS authenticated. Point back at the attack-surface trace: that whole vector was a valid login (authN passed) followed by lateral movement the permissions never should have allowed (authZ failed). Least privilege is the authZ control. Forward-reference: on Day 4 they will break an authorisation check directly — changing an ID in a URL to read another user's data (IDOR) is a pure authZ bug with authN fully intact. Finally mention ACCOUNTING / auditing as the third A: logs that attribute actions to identities. It is what makes an incident investigable, and it is the raw material for the SIEM work later in the week. Keep this slide to ~2 minutes; the factors slide is next.

Identity · how you actually prove it

Three kinds of proof — and why two beat one

Something you know

A password, a PIN, an answer. Copyable — and copied at scale.

Something you have

A phone, a security key, a card. Has to be physically present.

Something you are

Fingerprint, face, iris. Convenient — and impossible to change.

MFA is two proofs from two different rows — a password plus a second password is still one factor. Look back at where stolen credentials come from: phishing, breach dumps, guessing. Every one of them steals something you know. None of them puts the key in your pocket into the attacker's hand.
This is the slide that makes "turn on MFA" mean something. The three factor categories: SOMETHING YOU KNOW (password, PIN — the only one that can be stolen remotely, in bulk, without you noticing), SOMETHING YOU HAVE (phone, hardware security key, smart card — forces the attacker to be physically present or to steal a device), SOMETHING YOU ARE (biometrics — convenient, but note the sting: you cannot rotate a fingerprint after a breach, which is why biometrics usually unlock a local device rather than being sent to a server). MULTI-FACTOR means combining categories, not stacking items: password + security question is still single-factor, because both are things you know. Ask the room to connect it back two slides: phishing, breach dumps and weak-password guessing ALL attack the knowledge factor exclusively — so adding any second factor breaks the entire economy of credential stuffing, because a valid password on its own stops being enough. That is why MFA is the single highest-leverage habit, and it is exactly what the next slide asks them to go and switch on. If someone asks about phishing-resistant MFA: SMS codes can be intercepted or SIM-swapped, app codes can be phished in real time by a fake login page, but a FIDO2 security key (the blue token in the photo) is bound to the real site's domain and simply will not respond to the fake one — that is the gold standard. Keep it tight: name the three, land the "different rows" rule, then move.

Reality check · the defence that costs nothing

Make the leak worthless

Use a password manager

A unique password per site, remembered for you.

1Password · Bitwarden · Vaultwarden (self-host)

Turn on MFA

Blocks the login even with the right password.

Check for leaks

Is your email already in a breach?

haveibeenpwned.com

You can't stop leaks — you can make them worthless.
The fix, plainly. A password manager gives a unique password per site so one leak can't spread — 1Password (polished, paid), Bitwarden (free, open-source), or Vaultwarden (self-hosted Bitwarden server, for the keen). MFA defeats the login even when the password is correct — the single highest-leverage habit. And check exposure: Have I Been Pwned (haveibeenpwned.com) tells you if your email is in a known dump; managers also flag reused and breached passwords for you. Frame it as the action every student should take today.

Risk vocabulary · first principles · six words, one chain

Asset, threat, vulnerability, exploit — one chain

Asset what you defend

Anything with value: data, a system, a service, money, a person, a reputation. If it has no value, it needs no security.

Threat who or what

Any circumstance or actor that could harm the asset. The threat actor is the who; a flood or a failure is a threat too.

Vulnerability the weakness

A weakness that a threat could use. Note whose it is: a vulnerability is a property of your system, not of the attacker.

Exploit the weakness, used

The vulnerability actually being taken advantage of. Vulnerability = potential; exploit = it happening. (Also the name of the code that does it.)

Impact how bad

What it costs you if it lands: money, downtime, data, trust, lives.

Control what you add

Anything that lowers likelihood or impact — a patch, a lock, a policy, a backup, training.

Risk isn't an object — it's a measurement: Likelihood × Impact. That's why a rare catastrophe and a frequent nuisance can score the same, and why "is it vulnerable?" is the wrong question — the right one is how much risk, and is that more than we accept? Next slide: the same six words, told as one attack — on Superman.
Definitions first; the Superman slide is then pure illustration, and it lands far better once these words already mean something. Walk the chain left to right: a THREAT runs an EXPLOIT against a VULNERABILITY in an ASSET, producing IMPACT — and a CONTROL can break that chain at any link, which is the practical point of drawing it as a chain at all. Then the three distinctions students reliably get wrong, in order. (1) THREAT vs VULNERABILITY: the threat is external and you usually cannot remove it — you don't get to abolish criminals or storms — while the vulnerability is YOURS, which is precisely why almost all defensive work targets the vulnerability rather than the threat. (2) VULNERABILITY vs EXPLOIT: a vulnerability is a weakness that exists whether or not anyone has noticed; an exploit is that weakness actually being used, and "an exploit" is also the noun for the code that does it. An unexploited vulnerability is still a real risk — this is the answer to "but nobody has attacked us". (3) RISK is not a thing in the system, it is a measurement OF the system: Likelihood × Impact. Hammer the consequence, because it reframes the whole job: security is not "remove every vulnerability", which is impossible, it is "know your risks and spend where they are largest". Note also that ASSET comes first for a reason — you cannot rank risk until you know what is worth defending, which is why real programmes start with an asset inventory and not with tools. NIST's wording, if anyone wants it: a vulnerability is "a weakness that could be exploited or triggered by a threat source"; risk is "a function of the adverse impacts that would arise and the likelihood of occurrence". Keep it to three minutes, then let Superman do the remembering.

Risk vocabulary · one story, told in pictures

The whole vocabulary in one attack on Superman

Risk isn't one number — it's two. Likelihood (how often Lex can get his hands on kryptonite) × Impact (Metropolis falls) = Risk. Add the control and what's left is Residual risk — you drive it down, but never to zero.
And a control is only one of four things you can do with a risk: reduce it (the lead vault) · transfer it (insurance, or hand the job to someone who does it better) · avoid it (stop keeping the kryptonite at all) · accept it (eyes open, in writing). “Do nothing” is only a decision when it's the fourth one.
One attack, told in pictures — every term is a real image, not an icon. ASSET = the thing of value (Superman / Metropolis's protector). THREAT = the actor who wants to harm it (Lex). VULNERABILITY = the ONE weakness in the asset (kryptonite) — the lesson: the most powerful asset alive still has a single weak point, and security is about the weakness, not the strength. EXPLOIT = the weakness actually USED (Lex gets kryptonite in hand and wields it) — a vulnerability is only potential until someone exploits it. Then we RATE the risk with two independent dials, woven in the caption: LIKELIHOOD = how probable (how easily Lex obtains kryptonite); IMPACT = how bad if it lands (Metropolis falls); RISK = Likelihood x Impact — that is why a rare-but-catastrophic event and a frequent-but-trivial one can carry the same risk. CONTROL = anything that lowers likelihood OR impact (hide the kryptonite, lead-lined vault, Justice League on call). RESIDUAL RISK = what remains AFTER controls — kryptonite still exists somewhere, so you never reach zero; you manage it down to a level you accept. Land two ideas: attack the weakness not the strength; and risk has two levers that controls push on. Then the bottom line, briefly: adding a control is only ONE of the four legitimate responses to a risk — REDUCE (a control that lowers likelihood or impact), TRANSFER (insurance, or outsourcing the function to someone better at it — note you can transfer the cost, never the reputation), AVOID (stop doing the risky thing entirely: don't collect the data, retire the service), ACCEPT (a documented, deliberate decision that the residual risk is tolerable). The point students miss: ACCEPT is a real, professional answer — security is not "eliminate every risk", it is "spend where it pays". What is never acceptable is the fifth option, ignoring it, because that is accepting a risk without knowing you did.

Threat actors · who attacks, and why

Who's coming for you decides how you defend

Five actors, placed by CAPABILITY (x) and PERSISTENCE / how targeted (y) — position teaches. Click each face. SCRIPT KIDDIE (thrill): low skill, borrowed tools, sprays everyone — basics stop them. CYBERCRIMINAL (money): the fat middle, organised, crime-as-a-service — the one that actually hits most people; backups + MFA + phishing training. INSIDER (access): low tech but already trusted and INSIDE the perimeter — the twist; least privilege, monitoring, offboarding. HACKTIVIST (cause): defacement/DDoS/leaks aimed for publicity; harden public sites. NATION-STATE / APT (state): near-unlimited resources, patient, custom zero-days — you MANAGE the risk, rarely stop it. The takeaway to land: don't defend against a bored teenager the way you defend against a government — match the control to the actor. This sets up the rest of the week's tooling: every defence answers "which actor, going after what?"

What you built today

Confidentiality

Only the right people can read it.

Caesar → XOR/AES → RSA → Diffie–Hellman → the digital envelope. Keep the method public, the keyspace impossible — in transit (HTTPS) and at rest (disk, backups, database).

Integrity

The data is exactly what it should be.

SHA-256 fingerprints, checksums, salted+slow password hashes, signatures. A hash proves nothing changed; a signature adds who sent it and that they can't deny it.

Availability

Authorised users can reach it.

You watched a DDoS drop real users — and the defences that bring them back. A security property, not “IT’s problem”.

Beyond the triad: authentication asks who you are, authorisation what you may do — and MFA means two proofs from two different rows. Attackers log in, they don’t hack, so make the leak worthless.
