Day 5 · The Defender's Chair

For four days you were
the thing on the screen.

Every payload you fired this week landed in somebody's log. Today you sit on the other side of that glass. The job is not to know more tools than the attacker — it is to answer one question, over and over, under time pressure: is this thing I am looking at actually bad?

Set the chair-swap in one breath. They are not learning "defence" as a topic; they are learning a JOB — the SOC analyst's. Two promises for the day: (1) by lunch they will know what every tool in a SOC actually does, because they will have driven a small version of each; (2) after lunch they investigate a real breach, then defend live against each other. Do NOT let this become an acronym lecture — every tool today gets answered with the same question: what does it SEE, and what can it therefore never catch?

Every acronym today is just an answer to one of these four questions. For each one, we ask the same thing back: what does it see — and what can it therefore never catch?
This is the spine of the whole morning; put it up and keep referring back to it. Land three things. (1) Collection is upstream of everything — no telemetry, no detection, no investigation, full stop. That is the logging gap they will hit in this afternoon's lab, so plant it now. (2) Detection is a FILTER, not an oracle; it decides who gets woken up. (3) Triage is where the human lives — the tools do not answer "is this bad," they only narrow it down. Tell them explicitly: you are about to drive a small version of each tool. Do not memorise the acronyms; memorise the coverage question.

Detect · antivirus

AV asks one question: seen this exact file before?

Start with the oldest tool so everything after it has something to improve on. Drive it in three beats. (1) Press Scan file — the head walks the bytes, the hash lands, and it matches Trojan.Shell.Majal.a in the database. Say what just happened: AV read the file, hashed it, looked the hash up. It never ran anything. That is why it is cheap enough to scan every file on every machine. (2) Press Flip one byte — point out that the byte is inside a comment, "v2" to "v3": the shell never even executes it, the file does exactly the same damage. Scan again → CLEAN. Read the "59/64 hex digits changed" counter out loud and call back to Day 1's avalanche effect. (3) Let someone in the room click a random byte in the grid themselves. Land the sentence: a signature describes a file somebody already caught. Real AV does add heuristics and YARA-style string rules, which is worth one sentence — but the structural limit stands, and it is exactly why the next tool exists. Ask the room the handoff question before you advance: if renaming and recompiling defeat the hash, what should we watch instead?

Collect + detect · endpoint detection & response

EDR watches what a process does, and who started it

The answer to the question you just asked. Same attacker, same server, no malware file at all. Walk the escalation — each click is a beat, and the whole point is that the learner drives the attacker's side. (1) Run the attack as-is: php-fpm spawns sh spawns curl. Three alerts. Say it plainly — a web server has no legitimate reason to ever spawn a shell; that parent→child EDGE is the detection, and no file was scanned to find it. (2) Press rename the binary: the name-based rule goes grey, the lineage rule stays red. This is the beat to land hard — you cannot rename your way out of who your parent is. Note the AV panel too: it is right to allow the file; curl IS legitimate. This is "living off the land," and they will meet it by name in the lab. (3) run the tool directly — no shell, weaker signal, still a signal. (4) stay inside the app — the webshell opens the socket from inside php-fpm; both lineage rules go silent because there is no child process to see. (5) Add blend in: 443 → a cloud host — everything goes silent. Do not rush past this. Ask: "did we lose because our tool is bad?" No — the attacker gave up capability to get quiet, and that is the trade you are forcing them to make. Then hand off: the only thing that could still catch this is joining evidence from DIFFERENT sources, which is the next slide, and it is the whole reason SIEM exists.

Triage · security information & event management

A SIEM does not watch anything — it joins what others saw

Correct the mental model first: most students think SIEM = "log viewer with a search box." It is a join engine. Say the line on the slide out loud. Beats to drive. (1) As loaded, both rules fire and there are zero false alarms — walk the timeline and point out that no single dot is an alert. Forty failures is a bad password; one success is a Tuesday; a shell is a maintenance script. Only the join is a breach. (2) Switch auth.log off. The rules do not weaken, they go NO DATA. This is the most important click in the deck — collection is upstream of detection, and no clever rule recovers a source you never collected. Tie it to Stage 1 of the pipeline. (3) Switch it back on, then press low & slow: the identical attack spread over six days, every event still in the log, and the rule sees nothing. This is Hard mode ("The Slow Drip") in the afternoon lab. (4) Now let them fix it — widen the window and drop the threshold until it catches, and watch the false-alarm counter climb. That trade is the whole of detection engineering, and it sets up the next slide. Do NOT let anyone leave thinking a bigger window is free.

Triage · why tuning is the job

A 99%-accurate detector still buries you

The arithmetic behind the last slide's trade-off, and the most counter-intuitive thing in the day — do not skip it because it looks like a stats slide. Start at the default: 221 alerts, 3 real, one in 74. Then drag sensitivity to maximum: catch rate 99%, and precision collapses to 1 in 2,794 with 105 full-time analysts needed. Ask the room where the mistake is. There isn't one — that is what happens when you multiply a small false-positive rate by a huge event count. Say the general form: when the thing you are looking for is rare, even an excellent test is usually wrong when it fires. Then drag the other way and let them see the tempting trap: a quiet queue bought by missing half the attacks, and nobody ever files a ticket about the attack you did not detect. Land three consequences: (1) this is why alert fatigue is a real security failure, not laziness; (2) it is why correlation beats raw sensitivity — a join has a far better base rate than a single signature; (3) it is why this afternoon's scoring punishes confident wrong answers hardest. An analyst who invents a source IP is a false positive with a human attached.

Detect · intrusion detection & prevention

A network rule is a line of text — and you can edit it

Demystify the box: this is a real Suricata-shaped rule, and the detection is a string match. Beats. (1) Plain payload matches — note the sensor gets a copy; the attack already reached the server, so IDS gives you knowledge, not protection. (2) mIxEd CaSe → miss. Let someone suggest the fix, then click nocase. (3) URL-encoded → miss; click http.uri and explain normalisation — Suricata matches the decoded URI. This is the arms race made visible, and they are on the defender's side of it now. (4) UNION/**/SELECT → miss, and no option fixes it: /**/ is a comment to the database and a gap to the sensor. Same disease as the AV byte-flip, one layer up. (5) Switch to IPS and type select into the content box — the attacker is blocked and so is a shopper searching "select the right size guide". CUSTOMER LOST. This is why IPS runs in detect-only for weeks first: inline, a false positive is downtime, and it is the same trade they just made on the base-rate slide. (6) Finish with over HTTPS: every rule blind at once. Ask what is left — the endpoint, where the data is already decrypted. That is the arc of the last twenty years of this industry in one click.

Detect · data loss prevention

DLP guards the exit, and only knows what data looks like

The only tool today that watches data rather than behaviour. Beats. (1) Let the scan run and point at the highlights: national IDs, IBANs, card numbers. (2) Point at the green match — a 16-digit internal order reference that the card detector deliberately did not flag, because it fails the Luhn check. That checksum is real and it is running in the widget; it is the difference between a detector and a regex. Worth thirty seconds: this is how a rule earns the right to block. (3) Note that Bulk records alone would have caught this — volume is often the strongest signal, because nobody exports the whole customer table by accident. (4) Then press base64 and let it land in silence: byte-identical data, every detector at zero. Say clearly that base64 is not encryption — anyone can decode it in one command — but DLP matches shapes, and the shape is gone. (5) Switch to monitor only and connect it back: this is IDS vs IPS again. Detect is cheap, block is a promise. Honest framing to close: DLP is genuinely good against accidents and the careless insider, and weak against an attacker who knows it is there. That is still worth buying — most data loss is an accident.

Respond · security orchestration & automated response

SOAR is a runbook a computer executes at 3am

Deflate the acronym immediately: there is nothing intelligent here. Every step is something an analyst already does by hand — look up the IP, check what the machine is, find out whose account it is, then act. SOAR is that list, written down once. Beats. (1) Run it on the breach: contained in 8.4 seconds against a human's 41 minutes. The win is speed and consistency, not judgement — and consistency matters most at 3am on a Friday. (2) Switch to a false positive with the guardrail on: a nightly billing job on a tier-0 payment gateway. The playbook enriches everything and stops, handing the analyst a ticket that is already answered. Name this shape: good automation does the boring 90% and stops at the ten seconds of judgement. (3) Now turn the guardrail off and run it again — it isolates the payment gateway, checkout down 14 minutes, 312 orders lost. Let that sit. The lesson is not "automation is dangerous"; it is that automation amplifies whatever your logic already was, including the wrong parts, at machine speed and without anyone watching. (4) Point ahead: this afternoon, blue team calls out attacker IPs and a wrong call is friendly fire. Same rule, human version — evidence before action.

Synthesis · what you can actually prove

No single tool covers the chain

The payoff slide. Every tool they drove this morning is a row; the real Majal breach is the columns. Read a few rows out loud so the shape lands: EDR is blind to the login, the logs are blind to the beacon, DLP wakes up only at the very end. That picture is defence in depth — not a slogan, a matrix. Beats. (1) With everything on, point at column 2 sitting at zero. The victim typed their password into a page on the attacker's server; no tool you own can ever see that. Some blind spots are not budget problems. Connect it straight to the lab: "did the victim enter their credentials?" answers cannot be determined, and saying so scores +3 while guessing confidently scores −3. (2) Toggle sensors off one at a time and watch the questions on the right flip to NO. Frame coverage as a list of questions you can answer, not a score — and note you only find out what you failed to collect after the incident. (3) Finally press log rotation: seven minutes of ordinary maintenance, roughly 1,100 requests gone, and the IDOR becomes unprovable. Nobody attacked the logging. It rotated. They will hit this exact hole in about an hour — when they do, the correct answer is to say the data is gone, not to estimate.

Reference · the rest of the alphabet

Everything else you'll hear in an interview

XDR

EDR that also ingests network, email and cloud telemetry into one console. Mostly a product packaging word — treat claims with suspicion.

NDR

IDS's modern cousin: baselines behaviour on the network instead of matching payload signatures. Survives encryption better.

UEBA

Baselines each user and flags deviation — Maha never logs in from another country at 3am. Powerful and false-positive prone.

TIP

Threat intel platform. Stores known-bad IPs, hashes and domains so enrichment (SOAR step 2) has something to look up.

WAF

An IPS that only speaks HTTP, parked in front of the web app. Would have sat on Day 4's payloads — and can be bypassed the same ways.

CASB / DSPM

DLP and posture management for SaaS and cloud storage, where most company data now actually lives.

SOC tiers

L1 triages the queue, L2 investigates, L3 hunts and engineers detections. Most people start at L1 — that is today's lab.

MTTD / MTTR

Mean time to detect and to respond. The two numbers a SOC is actually judged on. Industry MTTD is still measured in days.

Every one of these is a variation on the four stages. If you can say what a tool sees and what it misses, you understand it — the acronym is just its marketing.
Deliberately fast — three or four minutes, no dwelling. The point is that nothing they hear in an interview should be a surprise word, not that they memorise these. Read each card's coverage sentence, not its definition. Two asides worth making: XDR is largely a vendor category, so being slightly cynical about it is the correct professional instinct; and UEBA is the honest answer to the "low and slow" attack from the SIEM slide, because a baseline does not care how slowly you move — at the cost of many more false positives, which they now know how to reason about. Finish on SOC tiers: L1 is a real, gettable first job, and the next four hours are exactly what it looks like.

Recap · the only question that matters

Six tools, one question asked six times

AVhas anyone seen this file?
Hashes a file and looks it up. Cheap, instant, and blind to anything one byte different — or to an attack with no file at all.
EDRwhat did the process do?
Watches behaviour and lineage. A web server spawning a shell is caught no matter what the binary is called — until the attacker stops spawning anything.
IDS / IPSwhat is on the wire?
Matches bytes in traffic. Defeated by a different spelling, and blinded entirely by encryption. IPS is the same thing allowed to block — and to break things.
DLPwhat does this data look like?
Recognises the shape of sensitive data leaving. Real checksums, real value against accidents — and base64 walks straight past it.
SIEMdo these events, together, mean something?
Joins sources nothing else can join. Cannot join what was never collected, and a time window is beatable by going slow.
SOARcan a computer do the first ten minutes?
Runs the runbook in seconds instead of an hour. It does not decide better — it decides the same way every time, so the logic had better be right.
You do not need to remember six definitions. Ask any tool: what does it see, and what can it therefore never catch? That question also tells you what a vendor is not saying.
Walk the levers, not the paragraphs — one line each, fast. The bolded limits are the actual content; the definitions they can look up. Then land the closing frame twice: (1) defence is a portfolio, because every tool has a shape it cannot see, and the coverage map is what that portfolio looks like; (2) the question in the callout is the transferable skill — it works on tools that do not exist yet, and it is the fastest way to see through a vendor pitch. Then hand over to the lab.

Day 5 · your shift starts now

The tools narrow it down.
They never decide.

You have driven every sensor a SOC owns, and you have seen each one go blind. What is left over — the judgement about whether this thing is actually bad — has your name on it. That is the job, and it starts now.

Next · the investigation. Sixteen thousand lines of it are your own attacks from this week. Almost everything hostile in that log was authorised. Three questions separate the noise from the breach: was the source authorised, was it inside the window, did it stay in scope?
And the rule that decides your score: a confidently wrong answer is worth less than “I don't know.” Some fields genuinely cannot be determined. Finding those is worth more than any other answer on the sheet.
Bookend to the title slide. Land the arc: they arrived able to break an app and are leaving able to say what a defender would have seen — and, more importantly, what nobody would have seen. Then set the lab up properly, because the scoring is the pedagogy. Say the confidence rule out loud and explain why it is not a gimmick: an analyst who invents a source IP sends the wrong person's laptop to be wiped, and that costs more than an honest "not sure". Point them at SCOPE.md first — rules of engagement before evidence, every time. Remind them the loudest thing in the dataset achieved almost nothing and the quietest thing is the actual breach. Then open console.html and let them work.
