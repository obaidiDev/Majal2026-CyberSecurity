Day 3 · Moving Through

One foothold isn't the prize —
the network is.

Yesterday you landed a single shell. Today you turn it into control of everything reachable. But first you have to speak the network's language — the real protocols underneath Day 1's diagrams. Learn the mechanism, then watch each one become a way through.

Frame the two acts. ACT 1: the network's language — the concrete protocols (ARP, ports/TCP/UDP, DNS, HTTP/TLS) that Day 1 deliberately left as a cartoon. ACT 2: moving through — every attack today weaponises a protocol they just learned. The spine of the day: learn the mechanism, then abuse it. They are still the attacker (Day 2's role), now inside the network with one low-privilege shell, aiming for the whole domain.

Act 1 · addressing & reach

Two halves of an address — and the wall called NAT

The foundation everything else stands on — do this before ARP. An IP address isn't one number, it's two: a network part and a host part, and the subnet mask is what says where the split falls. Walk the bits: at /24 the first 24 bits are the network, so every 192.168.1.x is on the same wire and you reach them directly (that's ARP, next slide). Slide to /25 and /26 and watch db01 and fileserver fall OUT of your subnet — still reachable, but only through the router, which can log, filter or refuse. That is segmentation, and it's the single most effective defence against the lateral movement we do in Act 2: it shrinks the blast radius of one compromised box. Name the switch/router distinction here: a switch moves frames inside one subnet using MACs; a router moves packets between subnets using IPs. Then flip to Attacker on the internet for the big land: 192.168.x.x is a private range — millions of networks use it, so the internet won't route to it. The router does NAT, which lets the office out and matches up the replies, but nothing outside can start a conversation in. Ask the room the question this answers: "if the office is unreachable, how did we get in on Day 2?" — you didn't knock, you got invited, by phishing. Every attack today only works because you're already on the wire.

Act 1 · addressing on the local wire

Two addresses: IP is who, MAC is where

The first mechanism. Every device carries TWO addresses: an IP (logical, end-to-end — Day 1's packet label) and a MAC (physical, burned into the network card, only meaningful on this local wire). To actually put a frame on the wire you need the destination's MAC — but you usually only know its IP. ARP bridges the gap. Drive the player: (0) you know .5's IP but not its MAC; (1) you BROADCAST "who has .5?" to everyone — dst MAC ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff; (2) only .5 replies with its MAC, the rest ignore it; (3) you cache the mapping; (4) now the real data frame goes straight to .5's MAC. The two things to land: (a) IP vs MAC — who vs where-on-this-wire; (b) the trapdoor — ARP is an unauthenticated broadcast: ANY machine can shout "that's me." Hold that; in Act 2 we become the liar (ARP spoofing). Real-world scale: a busy LAN resolves thousands of these a minute, all cached for ~seconds.

Act 1 · discovery · the quiet plumbing

Before you attack a host, you have to find it

DHCP your IP

Broadcast: "anyone got an address for me?" The reply hands you an IP, mask, gateway and DNS.

Trapdoor: trust-by-answer, like ARP — a rogue server names itself your gateway.

ICMP · ping alive?

Not a port, not a service — a control message. Echo request out, echo reply back.

But: many hosts drop ICMP on purpose. Silent is not dead.

Ping sweep the map

Ping every address in the subnet and list who answers.

Step 0 of every scan: hosts first, then ports — that's the next slide.

ARP scan on-wire

On your own subnet, ask by ARP. A host can ignore ping — it can't ignore ARP and still work.

So: ARP finds what ping misses.

traceroute the path

Packets with a deliberately short lifespan (TTL) — each router in turn reports itself.

Reveals: the hops between you and the target — and where the firewalls sit.
None of this is an exploit. Every one of these is a protocol doing exactly what it was designed to do — which is precisely why discovery is so hard to block.
A short, brisk slide — cards, not a toy, because each of these is a one-sentence idea. It sits here because discovery logically precedes the port scan: first which machines exist, then which doors each one has. Beats to land: (1) DHCP answers "where did my IP even come from" and completes the addressing story from two slides back — and note the rogue-DHCP trapdoor, the exact same trust-by-answer flaw as ARP and DNS; that pattern repeating three times is the real lesson of Act 1. (2) ping/ICMP — worth saying explicitly that ICMP is not TCP or UDP and has no ports; it's the network's own signalling. (3) ping sweep — the mental model of hosts-then-ports. (4) ARP scan — the nice attacker detail: a host can firewall off ICMP but cannot refuse ARP and still function. (5) traceroute — the TTL trick is genuinely clever, worth 20 seconds. Close on the callout: discovery abuses nothing, so defenders can't simply "patch" it — they can only segment and monitor.

Act 1 · ports & services

One IP, many doors — a port scan finds the open ones

An open port is a running service — a door with something behind it. Finding them is the attacker's first move once inside; each service maps to its own attack.
Now that you can reach a host (ARP got you its MAC), what can you talk to? A machine has one IP but 65,535 ports; a port that's listening = a running service = a door. Press Scan: the sweep probes every common port and each resolves open / closed / filtered. Land the mapping — every open port is a specific attack surface: 22 SSH → credential attack, 445 SMB & 3389 RDP → lateral movement (Act 2), 3306 MySQL → the data itself, 80/443 → the web app (Day 4). Then hit Toggle firewall: the sensitive internal services go filtered — the probe is dropped, you can't even tell what's there. That's the defender's lever: fewer open doors = smaller attack surface. This is nmap at toy scale; a real scan sweeps all 65,535 ports and fingerprints versions to match known CVEs.

Act 1 · connections

TCP shakes hands before it talks — UDP just fires

How do two machines actually hold a conversation? Switch between the three tabs and Play each. TCP: the 3-way handshake — SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK — builds a connection BEFORE any data; watch both sides walk CLOSED/LISTEN → ESTABLISHED. The payoff line: three packets before a single byte, and that cost buys reliability (ordering, retransmits). SSH, HTTP(S), RDP all ride TCP. UDP: no handshake, no state — packets just fly and a lost one is simply gone; great for DNS, voice, video. SYN scan: the attacker's trick — send only the SYN, read the reply (SYN-ACK = open, RST = closed), then send RST to bail before completing. That half-open scan is exactly how the port sweep two slides back found the doors — fast and quiet. Ties the connection mechanism to the recon they saw.

Act 1 · names → addresses

DNS: the internet's phonebook (and who it trusts)

Machines route to IPs, but humans remember names — DNS bridges the two. Press Resolve and watch the recursive walk: your resolver asks a root server (→ "ask .sa"), then the .sa TLD (→ "ask ns1.majal.sa"), then the authoritative server, which finally returns the IP. The resolver caches it — hit "Resolve again" to see the instant cache hit (no walk). Land two points: (1) it's a hierarchy of referrals, not one lookup; (2) the security gap that rhymes with ARP — DNS is trust-by-answer: whoever replies first is believed, so a forged reply (cache poisoning / spoofing) silently sends the victim to the attacker's server. Real scale: billions of queries/sec, cached at every layer. We won't build the spoof here — the ARP version in Act 2 makes the same point on the wire.

Act 1 · open a real packet

Wireshark: one login, every layer you learned

The payoff of Act 1 — Day 1 promised "Day 3 opens real packets in Wireshark," and here it is. This is a real capture of one login. Walk the packet list: notice packets 1–3 are the TCP handshake from two slides ago. Click packet 4 (POST /login) and open the dissection tree: it is literally every layer they learned as one row each — Ethernet (the MAC addresses ARP resolved), IP (the addresses), TCP (the ports that pick the service), then HTTP. Then the gut-punch: the hex pane on the right shows username=alice & password=Summer2026! in plain readable text. Now flip to HTTPS :443 and click the Application Data packet — same login, but TLS has sealed it; the bytes are opaque ciphertext, no password to see. Two lands: (1) a packet is just these nested headers you can read; (2) HTTP exposes everything to anyone on the wire, HTTPS doesn't — which is exactly the attack we run next. Bridge to Act 2: "so how does an attacker get onto the wire to read HTTP? Watch."

Act 1 · the lock, opened up

Why HTTPS beats you: certificates, not just encryption

The slide that answers the question the Wireshark slide raised and never explained: why is HTTPS opaque? Play it in Just the server first. ClientHello (nothing secret yet, all in the clear) → ServerHello + Certificate → the browser verifies it → key exchange → sealed Application Data. Land the one idea students usually miss: encryption is the easy half — knowing WHO you encrypted to is the hard half. Anyone can encrypt; the certificate is what binds a public key to the name "majal.sa", and a Certificate Authority's signature is what makes that binding believable. Day 1 already built the key exchange (Diffie–Hellman) — point at that step and say "this is that, running for real". Then switch to Attacker in the middle: you're ARP-spoofed into the path from the MITM slide, so you must terminate TLS yourself, which means handing alice a certificate for majal.sa. You can type the name, but you cannot forge a CA signature — so you self-sign, and the browser catches it instantly and throws NET::ERR_CERT_AUTHORITY_INVALID. Now make someone in the room click "Proceed anyway." Watch the password appear in your pane. The final land, and the best line of the day: TLS did not fail — alice overrode it. The last defence was a human decision, which is exactly Day 2's territory. This is also why browsers made that warning so ugly and hard to bypass, and why a cert warning on a public Wi-Fi network should end the session.

Act 2 · staying in touch · command & control

Your shell has to phone home

A firewall guards the door, not the exit. That asymmetry is why virtually every real implant is built to call out rather than be called.
The plumbing question Act 2 otherwise skips: you have a shell on web01 — but how are you still talking to it? Run all three. Bind shell: web01 listens on 4444 and you dial in — the obvious design, and it dies instantly, because inbound-deny is the one rule every edge firewall has (tie back to the firewall toggle on the port scan slide). Reverse shell: flip the arrow — web01 dials out to you, and the firewall waves it through, because outbound is how staff get work done. That inversion is the whole insight, and it's how nearly every real foothold works; Day 2's payload did exactly this. But 4444 outbound is odd-looking, so a defender watching egress can spot it. C2 over HTTPS: beacon out on 443 wrapped in TLS, at intervals. The firewall must allow 443, and the content is encrypted — note the irony out loud: the same TLS that protected alice two slides ago is now hiding the attacker. The tell is not the content but the rhythm — human browsing is bursty and irregular, a beacon is metronomic, and that timing signature is exactly what a defender hunts for on Friday. Real tools: Cobalt Strike, Sliver, Mythic; the "beacon" name comes from this behaviour.

Act 2 · orient · situational awareness

You land blind — enumeration turns on the lights

idYour user and groups. Are you already privileged? If not, escalation is next.
ip a  ·  arp -aYour subnet and the neighbours in your ARP cache. The map of who else to attack — straight from Act 1's ARP.
ss -tlnpServices listening on this box — including localhost-only ones a scan can't see. More doors, now from the inside.
sudo -lWhat you're allowed to run as root. The #1 lead for privilege escalation (next slide).
cat ~/.bash_history  ·  ls ~/.sshTyped commands, saved passwords, private keys. Often free credentials lying on disk.
ps aux  ·  crontab -lRunning processes and scheduled jobs. What's valuable here — and more escalation leads.
None of this is loud or clever — it's just looking around. But it's quiet (all local) and it feeds every other stage: escalation leads, credentials, and the next targets.
Stage 1 of the loop. When a shell lands you know almost nothing — enumeration is orientation. Don't drill every command; land the mindset: three questions — who am I (privileges), where am I (network + the ARP neighbours from Act 1), what's here (services, creds, jobs). Two takeaways: (1) it's all local and quiet, so it rarely alerts anyone; (2) it's not busywork — each command feeds a later stage (sudo -l → privesc, .ssh → loot, arp -a → lateral targets). This is a checklist attackers automate (tools like LinPEAS); we keep it to the six that matter.

Act 2 · sniff the wire · man-in-the-middle

Become the middle: ARP-spoof, then read the traffic

The two Act-1 mechanisms fired at once. Remember slide 2: ARP is an unauthenticated broadcast — anyone can lie. Here you do. First press Victim logs in while normal: the traffic goes straight to the gateway, your capture pane is empty (a switch only sends frames to their real destination). Now hit Spoof ARP: you send forged replies telling the victim "I'm the gateway" and the gateway "I'm the victim" — both cache your MAC, and the path visibly bends through you. Log in again: now the victim's packets cross your machine, and the capture pane shows the cleartext HTTP login — password=Summer2026!. Finally flip the victim to HTTPS: you're still in the middle, but you relay only encrypted Application Data — no password. Lands the whole arc: this is why HTTPS-everywhere matters, and why "inside the LAN" is not "safe." Real tools: Ettercap, Bettercap, arpspoof. This also seeds Day 5 — ARP spoofing is noisy and detectable, which the defender will catch.

Act 2 · escalate · low-priv → root

Privilege escalation: turn a misconfig into root

Stage 2 of the loop. You have alice's shell, not root's — escalation is abusing a misconfiguration to become root. Have them click each lead and read the terminal: SUID (a root-owned binary with the s-bit runs as root — abuse it), sudo NOPASSWD find (allowed to run find as root, and find can spawn a shell — GTFOBins), writable cron (a root job you can edit = scheduled root code execution). Then the teaching trap: /tmp writable — click it and it fails, staying alice. Land that: "writable" is not "exploitable"; it only matters if something privileged runs your file. The prompt flips alice$ → root#, uid 1000 → 0. Two lands: (1) privesc is misconfigurations, not 0-days; (2) the fix is hygiene — least privilege, audit SUID/sudoers. Real hunters automate this with LinPEAS/WinPEAS; every technique here is real (toy targets).

Act 2 · loot · credential access

Loot the credentials: crack them, or just reuse the hash

Passwords are stored as one-way hashes (Day 1) — but a weak one still falls to an offline dictionary in seconds, and for NTLM the hash itself logs you in.
Stage 3 of the loop. Bridge the OS jump explicitly: web01 is Linux, but it runs Samba to serve Windows file shares — and Samba keeps its own password database of NT hashes (the Windows format), which as root you dump with pdbedit. That's how a Linux box hands you Windows-style credentials. (On a real Windows host the equivalent is mimikatz against LSASS or the SAM.) These are the same real NTLM hashes as Day 1's fingerprints — the table computes them live. Press Run dictionary attack: it steps the wordlist, and for each candidate it actually hashes the word and compares. bob (sunshine), alice (Summer2026!) and svc_backup (P@ssw0rd!) match — land that even a "complex-looking" seasonal password with a symbol falls to word+rules. Point out the no-salt line: one hash of a candidate is tested against all four accounts at once, because NTLM adds no per-user salt — that's why it's so weak. The 16-char random administrator password never appears in the list, so it does NOT crack. So press Pass-the-hash: for NTLM/SMB the hash itself authenticates — you log in as administrator to the file server without ever knowing the password. Two lands: (1) password strength decides crack time — length/randomness matter (Day 1, from the attacker's side); (2) pass-the-hash means a strong password can't save an account once its hash leaks — that stolen credential is the fuel for lateral movement next.

Act 2 · the target · what "the domain" means

The domain: one login that opens every machine

The problem it solves

500 computers shouldn't mean 500 separate accounts. So the organisation runs one central directory — Active Directory — holding every user, group and machine.

You get: one username and password, and every company machine accepts it.

The Domain Controller

The server that is that directory. Every login in the building is checked by it.

Which means: it stores the password hash of every account in the organisation — including yours.

Domain Admin

A group whose members are administrator on every domain-joined machine at once.

Not "an admin somewhere" — the admin, everywhere, by design.

Why you aim here

From the DC you can reset any password, read any file share, and push software to every endpoint in the company.

Convenience for IT is the same thing as leverage for you.
This is why the loop ends at the Domain Controller. Every hop you make is really a hunt for one credential the whole domain trusts — and once you hold it, you stop hacking and simply log in.
A definition slide, deliberately not a toy — and it must come before lateral movement, because until now "domain admin" has been an undefined goal. Same discipline as Act 1: teach the mechanism before you weaponise it. Explain it from the IT side first so it doesn't sound sinister: managing hundreds of machines with local accounts is impossible, so Windows networks centralise identity in Active Directory. Then turn each convenience into leverage — (1) one credential works everywhere, so one stolen credential works everywhere; (2) the DC must know every hash in order to check every login, which makes it the single richest target in the building; (3) Domain Admin is a group, not a person, and membership means administrator on every joined machine simultaneously. Land the framing line: attackers don't break into the domain, they authenticate to it — the pass-the-hash from the last slide is the key, and the next slide is the lock. Mention that Linux/macOS shops have equivalents (LDAP, Kerberos realms, SSO/Entra ID) and the logic is identical: centralised identity is a huge security win and a huge single point of failure. Defences arrive Friday — tiered admin, separate admin accounts, and never letting a domain admin log into an ordinary workstation.

Act 2 · move · lateral movement

Spread: one box at a time, all the way to the domain

Stage 4 — the payoff of the loop. You own web01 (Day 2's foothold) and carry only the loot from the last two slides: alice's cracked password and the local Administrator NT hash. Click the glowing machines to move. Land the key distinction up front: that local-admin hash opens fileserver (the same local account, reused — a real and common technique) but the DC refuses it — a local admin is not a domain credential. So the DC stays locked until you go get one. The only path that yields it is a chain: dev-pc via alice's password (RDP), which drops svc_sql DB creds → db01 via those creds (MSSQL), where a Domain Admin has a live session you steal → only now does the DC light up. Make someone try the DC early — it refuses ("reachable, but you don't hold the credential it accepts"), which nails the rule: movement needs reachability + the right credential. The one big land: no single magic exploit — it's a chain of reused credentials hopping box to box, and one uniquely-strong local admin password would have broken it. That's why segmentation, unique passwords and LAPS matter (Day 5's defences). Real tools: PsExec, Evil-WinRM, CrackMapExec, RDP/SSH.

Act 2 · stay in · persistence

Persistence: make sure you can always come back

New account

Create a quiet admin user of your own.

Survives: the hole being patched

Scheduled job

A cron/task re-opens your shell every few minutes.

Survives: reboots, process kills

Boot service

Install a service that starts your implant at boot.

Survives: reboots

SSH key

Drop your public key in ~/.ssh/authorized_keys.

Survives: password resets & MFA

Web shell

Hide a one-line shell in the web app's files.

Survives: lost creds — reachable from anywhere
The foothold was expensive to win — persistence makes coming back cheap, even after a reboot, a patch, or a password reset. Note the SSH key and web shell: they outlast the very fixes a defender would apply first.
Stage 5 — locking in. Once inside, attackers plant multiple independent ways back so a single cleanup doesn't evict them. Don't drill each; land the pattern and the nasty part: several of these survive the obvious response — resetting the password doesn't remove an SSH key or a web shell; patching the entry hole doesn't remove a new admin account. That's why real incident response assumes persistence and hunts for these specific artifacts (new accounts, odd scheduled tasks, unexpected authorized_keys, files in web roots) rather than just closing the original door. This maps to MITRE ATT&CK's Persistence tactic — name it, move on. Seeds Day 5's "assume breach" mindset.

Act 2 · cash out · impact

The goal: take the data out — and make it hurt

1 · Collect the crown jewels

The customer database, source code, secrets, backups — whatever the domain protects. You now have the access to read all of it.

2 · Exfiltrate quietly

Ship it out disguised as normal traffic — encrypted HTTPS to a cloud drive, or trickled through DNS queries — so it blends in and slips data-loss filters.

3 · Monetise

Sell the data, or detonate ransomware: encrypt everything and threaten to leak what you stole — double extortion.

Exfiltration is the one loud-ish step — moving gigabytes out looks different from normal. It's also the defender's best late catch, which is exactly where the next slide picks up.
The "why" behind the whole day. Keep it brief — this is impact, not a new technique. Three beats: (1) with domain control they can reach the actual valuables; (2) smart exfil hides in plain sight — encrypted and shaped like ordinary web or DNS traffic to beat data-loss prevention (this is why "it's encrypted" cuts both ways — TLS also hides the attacker's theft); (3) the payday — data sale or ransomware with double extortion (encrypt + leak). Land the bridge: exfil is bulky and anomalous, so even an attacker who was quiet all day gets loud here — setting up "you left tracks." Don't dwell; the emotional weight (this is a real business-ending event) is enough.

The other side · you left tracks

Every move inside wrote a line in someone's log

The bridge to Day 5, mirroring Day 2's closer. Replay each inside-loop move and watch the defender's SIEM. The reveal: the two quiet moves — local enumeration and offline hash cracking — left NOTHING; they never touched the network. But every move that crossed the wire or used a credential lit an alert: the internal port scan (IDS), the ARP spoof (arpwatch sees the gateway's MAC change), the privilege escalation (EDR), the pass-the-hash login to the DC (identity analytics — anomalous auth), and the exfil (DLP). Land it: an attacker inside is loud the instant they act on the network — which is the defender's whole opportunity. Ties back to Day 2's passive/active asymmetry, and forward to Friday, when they sit at this exact console and reconstruct the entire week from these alerts. Ask the room: which single alert would you want to catch first?
