The Engagement

Majal Store is a small online shop — you can browse products, log in, check your orders, download receipts, leave reviews. It looks finished. It is not safe.

You have been hired to find out how unsafe. This is a black-box penetration test: you get the same access any customer gets — a browser and a login — and nothing else. No source code, no map, no list of what's wrong. Your job is to probe every corner of the application, find the ways it can be abused, and prove it.

Everything you learned this morning — the URL, the request, where input enters, what the client is allowed to touch, how the app talks to its database — is the toolkit. This afternoon you use it against a real target.

You Are the Pen Tester

This is the part that makes it a test and not a walkthrough: we are not telling you where the flaws are. A real client doesn't hand their attacker a checklist, and neither will we. You are working as a penetration tester — you map the app yourself, decide what's worth poking, and discover the weaknesses on your own.

What we will tell you: the assessment team has confirmed the application contains at least seven distinct, exploitable vulnerabilities, spread across its ordinary features. Some are found in seconds; some take patience; a few only open up once you've used another. Finding all seven is the goal.

💡 If you get stuck, that's expected — raise your hand. Your instructor will give you a nudge toward a feature worth a second look, one hint at a time. Struggling with it first is how it sticks.

Your Objectives

OBJ 1Map the application. Visit every page. Note every place you can send input — search boxes, forms, URLs, IDs, cookies — and every different role or account you can act as.

OBJ 2Test the inputs. Take each input you found and ask what the app assumes about it — then break that assumption. Watch closely how the app responds.

OBJ 3Find and exploit the flaws. Locate at least seven distinct vulnerabilities and actually demonstrate each one — not "this looks off," but a working exploit that proves impact.

OBJ 4Escalate and chain. Some findings are keys to others. When one weakness gives you access, ask what that access unlocks next.

OBJ 5Write it up. Record each finding as you go — see the deliverable below. If it isn't written down, it didn't happen.

Your Missions

Objectives tell you how to work; these missions are the wins to capture. Each one is a goal, not a hint — how you pull it off is the whole point. They roughly build on each other, so work down the list, and don't move on until you can actually prove the one you're on.

MISSION 1Get in as the administrator. Reach the store's admin-only area — without anyone handing you the admin password.

MISSION 2Run your own command on the server. Make the machine hosting the store execute a command you chose, and get it to tell you which user the application runs as.

MISSION 3Capture the flag. Somewhere on that server sits a file named FLAG.txt that no page links to. Read it and recover the MAJAL{…} flag inside.

MISSION 4Open a stranger's order. View the full details — items, total, buyer — of an order that does not belong to your account.

MISSION 5Weaponise a link. Craft a single URL that, the moment someone opens it, runs JavaScript of your choosing in their browser.

MISSION 6Attack the whole room. Leave something on a product page that runs in the browser of every visitor who views it afterwards — including the person sitting next to you.

MISSION 7Hijack an account from afar. Build a page that, when a logged-in customer simply opens it, silently changes their account details — no password, no clicks on anything they'd suspect.

MISSION 8Chain them. Find a place where one of your wins becomes the key to another you couldn't reach directly — then make sure all of it is written up in your report.

💡 That's eight missions covering the assessment. If a mission has you stumped, that's the job — try a few angles before you raise your hand for a nudge.

Setup — Getting Connected

The target

The store runs on a shared box on the lab network. Your instructor will give you its address on the whiteboard — you'll browse to something like http://<lab-address>/. Everyone in the room hits the same target, so keep an eye on the site: other people's attacks are landing there too.

Your account

You'll be handed a personal login (username and password) for the store. There is no sign-up page — you're testing an existing app with the access a real, logged-in customer would have. Start there and see how far past your own account you can get.

What you need

  • A web browser with its developer tools (F12) — this alone is enough to complete the whole assessment.
  • Optional power tools, if you want them: an intercepting proxy (Burp Suite or OWASP ZAP), curl for hand-crafting requests, and content/parameter discovery tools. None are required.

Using Burp Suite (optional)

Burp lets you catch every request the browser sends, then replay and tamper with it — which is exactly what these missions call for. Because the store is plain HTTP, there's no certificate to install. Setup:

  1. Start Burp; its proxy listens on 127.0.0.1:8080 by default.
  2. Send your browser through it — either use Burp's built-in browser (Proxy → Open Browser), or point your browser's proxy at 127.0.0.1:8080.
  3. Browse to the store. Requests appear under Proxy → HTTP history.
  4. Right-click any request → Send to Repeater to edit and resend it by hand, or Send to Intruder to try many values automatically.

The free Community Edition is plenty (Intruder just runs slower). Explore the right-click menu on a captured request — Burp has tools that build whole attacks for you, once you work out which attack you need.

Can't reach the shared box? Ask your instructor for the offline fallback — a self-contained Docker image of the same store you can run on your own laptop and attack locally.

Rules of Engagement

⚠️ The target is the store, and only the store. Scanning, connecting to, or attacking anything else — a classmate's laptop, the instructor's machine, the wider network, the internet — is out of scope and ends your session. What you're doing is legal because you have explicit permission to attack this one deliberately vulnerable target. That permission does not extend one inch past it. This is the exact line the ethics slide draws today.

  • The box is shared and self-healing. It resets to a clean state every few minutes. If you knock it over — or someone else does — it comes back on its own. Don't count on your changes sticking around.
  • Griefing is still the lesson. If your attack breaks the site for the whole room, notice why — that's a finding worth writing down, not just a prank.
  • Work like a professional. A real tester documents, stays in scope, and doesn't destroy what they don't have to. Same here.

How to Work the Target

You're not guessing randomly — you're applying a method. Some prompts to keep moving, without giving anything away:

  • Enumerate every input. This morning's "five places your input enters" is your checklist. If the app reads it, test it.
  • Question what the client controls. URLs, IDs in the address bar, hidden form fields, cookies — anything that reaches you can be changed before it goes back. What does the server simply trust?
  • Read the responses. Error messages, status codes, and page contents often reveal more than the app intended. Feed it something malformed and listen.
  • Cross the line between accounts. Once you can act as yourself, ask what stops you from acting as — or seeing — someone else.
  • Chain your wins. A small foothold in one feature is often the key that opens a bigger door somewhere else.

Deliverable — Findings Report

Keep a running report as you go. For each vulnerability you find, record these five things — the same shape a real pentest report takes:

FieldWhat to write
WhereThe exact page / URL / parameter or input.
WhatThe class of flaw, in your own words.
ProofThe precise steps or payload you used to exploit it.
ImpactWhat an attacker gains — whose data, what access, what damage.
FixHow you'd stop it. (You'll defend against these tomorrow.)

Seven findings, fully written up, is a complete assessment. Bring it to the debrief — we'll compare what the room found.

Into Day 5

Every payload you send this afternoon lands in a log somewhere. Tomorrow you take the defender's chair and meet this same store from the other side — reconstructing an attack from the alerts it left behind. The cleaner your findings report today, the sharper your eye tomorrow.
